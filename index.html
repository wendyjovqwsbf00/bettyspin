How the peg board works

The board is built as a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row slightly wider than the one above it. A ball launched from the top has an even chance of going left or right at every peg, and that pattern repeats as it falls. When it reaches the bottom, it lands in one of the pockets arranged across the base — one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because each bounce is a fresh random split, most balls drift toward the middle and only a few reach the outer edges. Mathematically, this is the same logic behind a Galton board and a binomial curve. In practice, that means the centre pockets are hit more often, while the side pockets carry the bigger payouts.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for the final result.
Rows
The number of peg levels the ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that spreads the payout table wider or keeps it flatter without changing the core return much.
House margin
The small gap between the true odds and the payout values paid to the player.
Provably fair
A system that uses published hashes so the round can be checked after play.
Variance
The scale of wins and losses around the average result, which is what the risk setting mainly changes.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each bounce is driven by an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify the result after the round.

Do more rows improve returns?

No. Extra rows add pockets and reshape the odds curve, so the edge prizes get larger while their chance gets smaller. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is best?

There is no mathematically best choice. Low risk smooths the action, while high risk puts more value into rare outer pockets. The right setting depends on your budget and tolerance for swings.
